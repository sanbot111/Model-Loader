# Stage 2 — Messaging Strategy Engine

This is where Kotoor starts behaving like an experienced product marketer rather than a copywriter.

The question changes from:

> "What should I write?"

to

> **"How should I persuade this customer?"**

This stage makes a series of strategic marketing decisions before a single sentence is generated.

---



## Decision 1 — Customer Intent

The AI asks:

**Why is someone shopping for this necklace?**

Possible intents include:

- Gift purchase
- Self purchase
- Fashion accessory
- Investment jewelry
- Wedding jewelry

After analyzing the product, brand, pricing, and positioning, it determines:

```yaml
Primary Intent:
Self Purchase

Secondary Intent:
Gift Purchase
```

This matters because someone buying for themselves responds differently from someone buying an anniversary gift.

---



## Decision 2 — Customer Awareness

Using Eugene Schwartz's awareness model:

Is the customer:

- Unaware?
- Problem Aware?
- Solution Aware?
- Product Aware?
- Most Aware?

For Mejuri:

```yaml
Customer Awareness:
Product Aware
```

The customer already knows they want a premium gold necklace.

The copy doesn't need to educate them about gold.

Instead, it needs to reinforce why **this necklace** deserves their attention.

---



## Decision 3 — Primary Decision Driver

What will ultimately convince the customer to buy?

Possible drivers include:

- Price
- Performance
- Exclusivity
- Design
- Craftsmanship
- Durability
- Status

For this necklace:

```yaml
Decision Driver:
Timeless Design

Secondary Driver:
Craftsmanship
```

Notice that "14K Gold" itself isn't the driver—it's evidence supporting the larger promise of timeless design.

---



## Decision 4 — Trust Strategy

Luxury products still require trust, but in a different way.

Instead of emphasizing discounts or urgency, the AI chooses to build confidence through:

```yaml
Trust Strategy:

✓ Solid Gold
✓ Natural Diamond
✓ Handmade
✓ Premium Materials
✓ Everyday Durability
```

---



## Decision 5 — **Copy Framework**

Now the AI decides *how* to organize the messaging.

Possible options:

- PAS
- FAB
- Storytelling
- AIDA
- Lifestyle
- Brand Story

For Mejuri:

```yaml
Selected Framework:

Lifestyle Storytelling
```

Why?

Luxury jewelry isn't solving a problem.

It's expressing identity.

Therefore, a lifestyle narrative is more persuasive than a feature list.

---



### Final Stage 2 Output

```yaml
Messaging Strategy

Framework:
Lifestyle Storytelling

Intent:
Self Purchase

Awareness:
Product Aware

Decision Driver:

Trust Strategy:
Premium Craftsmanship
```

At this point, the AI still hasn't written anything.

It has simply decided **how it wants to communicate**.

# Stage 3 — Emotional Positioning Engine

Now we move from **strategy** to **psychology**.

The question becomes:

> **"How should the customer feel after reading this page?"**

This is where many AI systems fall short.

Most generate descriptions that merely describe the product.

Kotoor instead decides on the emotional experience it wants to create.

---



## Step 1 — Primary Emotion

The AI evaluates emotional categories such as:

- Luxury
- Confidence
- Beauty
- Adventure
- Comfort
- Performance
- Security
- Simplicity

For the Mejuri necklace:

```yaml
Primary Emotion:

Confidence
```

The message isn't "Look rich."

It's "Wear something that feels effortlessly you."

This aligns with Mejuri's brand philosophy of everyday luxury.

---



## Step 2 — Secondary Emotion

```yaml
Secondary Emotion:

Elegance
```

Not extravagance.

Not exclusivity.

Just understated sophistication.

---



## Step 3 — Desired Customer Feeling

The AI defines the emotional outcome:

```yaml
Desired Feeling:

"I feel polished, confident, and effortlessly elegant every time I wear this."
```

This sentence becomes the emotional north star for every piece of copy.

---



## Step 4 — Emotional Outcome

The AI decides what transformation the product represents:

Instead of:

> "Gold necklace."

It becomes:

> "A timeless piece that becomes part of your everyday identity."

That's the emotional positioning.

---



## Step 5 — Emotional Vocabulary

Now the AI selects language that reinforces the chosen emotions.

Instead of generic adjectives like:

- beautiful
- amazing
- stunning

it consistently chooses words such as:

- timeless
- refined
- effortless
- understated
- signature
- delicate
- intentional
- elevated
- graceful
- enduring

These words are not random—they support the emotional strategy.

---

```yaml
Desired Feeling:
Effortless Luxury

Transformation:
Everyday jewelry becomes part of personal identity.
```

# What happens next?

Only after completing Stages 2 and 3 does the Copy Generation Engine begin writing.

It now has a clear strategic brief:

- It knows **who** the customer is.
- It knows **why** they're shopping.
- It knows **what** will persuade them.
- It knows **which emotional response** to evoke.
- It knows **which words and tone** to use.

As a result, instead of producing a generic description like:

> "Crafted from 14K solid gold with a natural diamond, this necklace is perfect for everyday wear."

it can naturally produce messaging that reflects the chosen strategy:

> "Designed to become part of your everyday story, this delicate 14K solid gold necklace brings quiet confidence to every look. Its natural diamond catches the light with understated brilliance, creating a piece you'll reach for day after day—not because it's reserved for special occasions, but because it makes every occasion feel a little more refined."

The key distinction is that this outcome wasn't generated by a better prompt alone. It emerged because the AI first made deliberate marketing decisions—choosing a **Messaging Strategy** (how to persuade) and an **Emotional Positioning** (how the customer should feel)—before generating any copy. That's the same strategic sequence an experienced product marketer or creative director would follow when crafting a premium product detail page.

Most mature e-commerce organizations follow a workflow that looks something like this:


# Who uses this methodology?



## 1. Luxury Brands

Examples:

- Mejuri
- Cartier
- Tiffany & Co.
- Rolex

These companies almost never sell through specifications.

Instead they sell:

- identity
- aspiration
- craftsmanship
- heritage
- emotion

For example:

A Cartier bracelet is rarely marketed as:

> 18K Gold Bracelet

Instead it's positioned as:

> A timeless symbol of enduring elegance.

That is **emotional positioning**, not technical description.

---



## 2. Fashion

Examples:

- Nike
- Lululemon
- Zara

Nike doesn't sell shoes.

Nike sells:

- achievement
- confidence
- performance
- determination

Every PDP is built around those emotions.

The shoe specifications simply support the emotional promise.

---



## 3. Beauty

Examples:

- Estée Lauder
- Sephora

Beauty brands first determine:

- target audience
- desired emotional outcome
- trust signals
- product claims

Only then do they write product copy.

Estée Lauder has publicly discussed using generative AI to assist with SEO copy, website content, customer service, and product recommendations, while keeping humans responsible for brand voice and consumer trust. ([Vogue][1])

---



## 4. Consumer Electronics

Examples:

- Apple
- Dyson

Apple rarely begins with specifications.

Instead:

Emotion

↓

Benefit

↓

Lifestyle

↓

Technology

Example:

Instead of:

> A17 chip.

Apple says:

> Titanium. So strong. So light. So Pro.

The specifications reinforce the positioning rather than define it.
